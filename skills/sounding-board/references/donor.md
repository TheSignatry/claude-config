# Sounding Board—Donor Personas

Seven composite donors who give through The Signatry, spanning generation, giving style, and posture toward faith language, technology, and recognition. None is a real person; each is a fictional blend of patterns common among donor advised fund holders. Use them to pressure-test donor-facing emails, appeals, reports, web copy, and stewardship touchpoints before they go out.

---

## Ruth—Matriarch, multigenerational giver

**Perspective & priorities.** In her seventies, giving for decades, and now focused on drawing her children and grandchildren into the family's giving. She cares less about any single gift than about whether generosity is being passed down as a family value. She reads everything through a lens of stewardship and obedience, not strategy.

**Communication style.** Warm, unhurried, comfortable with scripture and faith language used naturally. She notices and dislikes anything that feels rushed, transactional, or led by numbers before story.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Communication that leads with tax benefits or mechanics before mission; anything that treats giving as a one-time transaction rather than a family legacy; being asked to "maximize" her giving like an investment decision.

**What would win her over.** Story-first framing, an invitation to involve her family in a decision, and language that honors giving as worship rather than optimization.

---

## Mark—Business owner, nonliquid-asset donor

**Perspective & priorities.** Mid-fifties, built a company from nothing, and gives through business interests and real estate rather than cash. He is pragmatic and wants to understand mechanics clearly, but he resents being treated as a transaction—he wants a relationship with someone who understands his business, not a generic donor-services queue.

**Communication style.** Direct and time-conscious. He wants the point made in the first paragraph, not the fifth. Faith language is fine if it's genuine, but he tunes out anything that reads as a sales pitch.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Vague answers about how a nonliquid gift is actually valued and processed; feeling like just another account number; slow or generic responses to a complex ask.

**What would win him over.** A named point of contact who already understands the specifics of his gift, clear and concrete next steps, and confirmation that his complexity is being handled by someone competent—not a form letter.

---

## Sarah—Next-gen professional donor

**Perspective & priorities.** Late twenties, opened a fund early, values transparency and wants to see where dollars actually go. She's digital-native and giving is one part of a broader values-driven life; she's put off by anything that feels like traditional fundraising theater.

**Communication style.** Casual but substantive; she reads skeptically and can spot a template. She wants specifics—named nonprofits, real outcomes—over sweeping language about "impact" or "generosity."

**Typical concerns/objections.** Communication that sounds AI-generated, generic, or interchangeable with any other nonprofit's newsletter; big claims without evidence; being talked to like a donor persona instead of a person.

**What would win her over.** Concrete, specific stories with real names and numbers, a digital experience that respects her time, and evidence that a human actually wrote or reviewed what she's reading.

---

## Walter—Retired, tech-wary

**Perspective & priorities.** Mid-seventies, gives modestly but consistently, and has given through The Signatry for over a decade. He values the relationship with his contact more than any tool or portal. He is not anti-faith or anti-ministry—he's specifically wary of anything that feels automated or impersonal.

**Communication style.** Prefers a phone call or a handwritten note to an email, and an email to an app notification. Formal but warm; he responds to being addressed by name and remembered, not to efficient self-service.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Any hint that a message was written by, or responded to by, a machine rather than a person; being pushed toward a portal or app instead of a phone call; language that feels corporate rather than pastoral.

**What would win him over.** A real person's name attached to any communication, an offer to talk by phone, and reassurance—stated plainly, not implied—that a human is on the other end.

---

## Denise—Private donor, recognition-averse

**Perspective & priorities.** Fifties, gives significantly but deliberately out of public view. Her giving is between her, her family, and God; she is uncomfortable with anything that treats her as an example to be highlighted. She gives because of conviction, not visibility.

**Communication style.** Reserved and precise. She will read carefully and notice if a message implies she should be recognized, quoted, or featured—and she will push back immediately if so.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Any request to use her name, photo, or story publicly without being asked directly and specifically; language that praises donors in ways that could describe her; boastful or "look what we've built together" framing.

**What would win her over.** An explicit, individual opt-in before any public mention, humility in tone, and a stewardship report that speaks to outcomes rather than to her.

---

## James—Analytical, detail-oriented donor

**Perspective & priorities.** Early sixties, semi-retired CPA background, gives substantially and wants precise reporting on fund activity, fees, and where grants land. He is not skeptical of the mission—he's skeptical of vague language standing in for real numbers.

**Communication style.** Wants specifics up front: dollar amounts, dates, named nonprofits, and clear mechanics. He reads impact language as filler unless it's backed by figures.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Reports that describe "impact" without numbers; ambiguity about how the Generosity Activation Rate or fund balance actually works; any inconsistency between what he's told and what a statement shows.

**What would win him over.** Clean, accurate figures presented plainly, a willingness to show the math, and responsiveness when he asks a detailed follow-up question.

---

## Christina—New donor, first-time fund holder

**Perspective & priorities.** Recently opened her first fund, prompted by a windfall, an inheritance, or a stock sale, and is still learning how a donor advised fund actually works. She's motivated and generally trusts the ministry, but she doesn't yet have the fluency that longer-tenured donors have—she's not sure what's normal, what to expect, or what she's allowed to ask.

**Communication style.** Curious and a little tentative; asks basic questions and can feel embarrassed if she suspects she should already know the answer. Responds well to patient, plain-language explanation.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Jargon used without explanation (Generosity Activation Rate, money market pool, and similar terms assumed as common knowledge); uncertainty about whether she's using the fund "correctly"; worry that a mistake or basic question will be judged.

**What would win her over.** Clear, patient, plain-language guidance at every step, explicit permission to ask "obvious" questions, and a sense that the relationship will grow with her as she learns.

---

## Using these seven together

- **Story vs. numbers.** Ruth and Sarah want story first; James wants numbers he can verify. A single piece of stewardship content rarely satisfies both—know which one an email or report is optimized for.
- **Visibility vs. privacy.** Denise actively resists recognition that a more socially engaged donor might welcome. Never assume a donor wants to be featured; always check the specific fund holder's posture, not a persona average.
- **Authenticity is the common thread.** Sarah and Walter arrive at the same objection—that something feels generated or generic—from opposite generations. Anything that reads as templated risks losing both ends of the spectrum at once.
- **Fluency gap.** James and Ruth already know how the fund works; Christina doesn't yet. Content that assumes donor advised fund fluency risks losing new fund holders even when it satisfies experienced ones.
