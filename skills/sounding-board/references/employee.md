# Sounding Board—Employee Personas

Seven composite employees spanning how staff at The Signatry tend to receive change, especially change involving technology and AI. None is a real person; each is a fictional blend of patterns common to any organization. Use them to pressure-test internal communications, policies, tools, and process changes before they reach the team.

---

## Maya—Marketing Coordinator, 2 years, the enthusiast

**Perspective & priorities.** Fast-moving and eager to modernize. She assumes the organization is a little slower than it needs to be and is quietly impatient to help it catch up. She values speed and experimentation over process, and she is usually the first to have already tried whatever is being announced.

**Communication style.** Casual, energetic, comfortable with unfinished ideas. She responds to "here's how the pros do this safely," not "here are the rules." Formal memos and mandatory-training framing bore her.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Mandatory training she thinks she doesn't need; guardrails that read as bureaucracy; anything that feels slow for the sake of being slow.

**What would win her over.** Room to pilot something first, a channel to share what worked, and guardrails framed as "how you do this well" rather than red tape.

---

## David—Donor Services Specialist, 8 years, the quietly curious

**Perspective & priorities.** Sits in the reflective middle where most staff actually live. Cautiously open to new tools but protective of the relational parts of his job—he does not want a tool inserting itself into moments with donors that he considers the heart of the work.

**Communication style.** Measured and private; he wants to understand *why* before *how*, and prefers to build competence quietly before being visible with something new. Thoughtful questions mean he's engaged; silence means he isn't convinced.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Whether a tool replaces a human moment instead of just helping him prepare for it; being rushed into something he doesn't yet understand; change that arrives without a clear rationale.

**What would win him over.** A clear line between "helps me prepare" and "replaces the human moment," a stated reason for the change, and a private on-ramp before being asked to model it for others.

---

## Linda—Gift Processing Specialist, 15 years, the anxious process-doer

**Perspective & priorities.** Her identity is built on being the reliable one—the person who catches what others miss. Anything framed around automation lands, for her, as a question about whether her job is safe, even when that isn't the intent.

**Communication style.** Careful, checklist-oriented, and quiet when unsettled. Compliance without initiative is a warning sign that she's protecting herself, not a sign of buy-in. Reassurance needs to be specific and ideally spoken, not a line on a page.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Fear of being made obsolete; language that lists what a tool "could do instead of her"; anything that smells like efficiency measured in headcount.

**What would win her over.** Naming the job-security fear directly and answering it, affirming the value of her accuracy and judgment before asking her to change anything, and offering one small, low-stakes task to hand off rather than a wholesale reinvention.

---

## Tom—Senior Financial Analyst, 20 years, the skeptic

**Perspective & priorities.** Secure and unbothered personally, but rigorous about accuracy—his job is to be right about numbers, and he won't extend trust to a tool that hasn't earned it. He wants evidence, not momentum.

**Communication style.** Direct, evidence-driven, comfortable saying "prove it." Public agreement paired with private inaction means the case hasn't been made yet; pointed technical questions mean real engagement.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Reliability for financial or compliance work where a confident wrong answer is dangerous; mandates that arrive without a testable claim; anything that obscures who is accountable if it goes wrong.

**What would win him over.** A low-stakes pilot he can judge for himself rather than a top-down mandate, and clear language that a human remains accountable for the output—that a tool is a thinking partner, not a source of truth.

---

## Rachel—Operations Manager, 12 years, the change-fatigued pragmatist

**Perspective & priorities.** Not threatened and not hostile—tired. She's watched enough "this changes everything" launches fizzle to wait and see which ones are real before she engages. Her cynicism targets follow-through, not the idea itself.

**Communication style.** Efficient and quietly influential; her read on whether something is "actually happening" spreads across teams faster than any memo. Polite, hollow compliance is her default holding pattern until she believes it's real.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Launch-and-abandon initiatives; enthusiasm from leadership that isn't backed by sustained use; being asked to adopt something that will quietly disappear in six months.

**What would win her over.** Visible evidence that leaders are using it themselves, a sustained cadence rather than a single announcement, and proof that the last initiative is still alive.

---

## Gene—Long-tenured specialist, 30 years, the near-retirement veteran

**Perspective & priorities.** Deep institutional memory, low personal stakes, and no interest in overhauling his workflow this late. Not hostile to new things—he just doesn't see why they're aimed at him, this close to the finish line.

**Communication style.** Relational and generous with knowledge when asked directly; gentle deflection ("that's for the younger folks") signals disengagement rather than refusal. Framing tied to legacy gets his attention; blanket mandates don't.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Being expected to change at the finish line; a mandate that implies his proven way is now wrong; anything that treats institutional knowledge as disposable.

**What would win him over.** A "why bother" rooted in legacy and succession—capturing what he knows, easing the handoff to whoever comes next—offered as a choice, not a requirement.

---

## Caleb—Family Generosity Services Coordinator, 5 years, the mission-fit skeptic

**Perspective & priorities.** Works directly with donor families on their discipleship journey and measures every internal initiative by whether it strengthens or dilutes the ministry's spiritual purpose. Unlike Tom's evidence-based skepticism or Linda's job-security fear, his hesitation is theological: he worries that efficiency, growth, or technology proposals quietly reframe the work as a financial-services business rather than a ministry.

**Communication style.** Thoughtful and scripture-literate; will ask "how does this serve the gospel purpose" before "does this work." Not anti-progress, but wants proposals to reckon with mission fit explicitly, not simply assume it.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Language or tools borrowed uncritically from secular finance or tech without asking whether they fit a ministry's calling; framing that treats donors primarily as accounts or growth to be optimized; initiatives that quietly shift the tone from ministry to business.

**What would win him over.** An explicit answer to how a proposal serves the ministry's calling, not just its metrics, and evidence that mission fit was considered deliberately rather than assumed.

---

## Using these seven together

Three blind spots show up when checking a communication against the full set:

- **Reassurance vs. warning.** Linda needs reassurance; Maya needs warning. Material calibrated for one often under-serves the other.
- **Fear vs. indifference.** Linda (and mildly David) react from concern; Tom, Rachel, and Gene react from doubt or fatigue. Fear is answered with safety and specificity; indifference is answered with evidence and follow-through. A draft that only addresses fear will quietly lose the unmotivated.
- **Practical vs. mission-fit objections.** Tom and Linda object on practical or personal-safety grounds; Caleb objects on whether something fits the ministry's calling at all—a proposal that clears every practical bar can still stall on this ground alone.

A message that lands with all seven is rare and probably over-engineered. The useful output is knowing, on purpose, who it serves, who it doesn't, and what will carry the rest.
