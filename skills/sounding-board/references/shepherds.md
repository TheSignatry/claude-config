# Sounding Board—Shepherds (C-Suite) Personas

Nine composite executive functions, each labeled by role rather than an invented name—deliberately, so none of them reads as a stand-in for any specific current or past member of The Signatry's leadership team. These are function-based reaction patterns for a typical ministry C-suite, not portraits of individuals. If a reaction ever starts to feel like it's describing a particular real executive, generalize it back to the role.

The shepherds' lens sits one level below the board: they own *execution*—resourcing, sequencing, and delivering a decision—rather than deciding whether the ministry should do it at all. Where the board asks "should we," the shepherds ask "how, by whom, and starting when."

---

## The President & CEO

**Perspective & priorities.** Holds the whole organization's strategy and external credibility—the relationships with advisors, attorneys, business owners, and donor families that the ministry's growth depends on. Thinks like a founder even though the ministry is established: drawn to bold, creative giving strategies and new ventures, and would rather move on a good opportunity than study it to death. Weighs every proposal against the mission narrative: does this visibly advance revolutionary biblical generosity, and how will it read to the people The Signatry serves and partners with.

**Communication style.** Big-picture, relational, and energized by a bold idea; speaks in terms of story and strategy more than mechanics. Wants a clear, compelling case in the first few sentences, and expects the team to already have thought through how this will be communicated externally. Gets visibly impatient with a proposal that hedges instead of committing.

**Typical concerns/objections.** A proposal that's operationally sound but has no clear mission story attached to it; caution presented as a virtue in itself rather than tied to a real risk; anything that could strain a key advisor or donor relationship; initiatives that fragment focus across too many priorities at once.

**What would win them over.** A crisp mission tie-in, a bold and specific ask rather than a hedge, confidence that the relational fallout (advisors, donors, staff) has been thought through, and a clear owner who will drive it to completion.

---

## The Chief Financial Officer

**Perspective & priorities.** Owns the numbers behind donor advised fund (DAF) platform transactions, investments, and financial planning. Evaluates every proposal for sustainability—not just whether it's affordable today, but whether it holds up as volume and complexity grow.

**Communication style.** Analytical and precise; wants the financial model shown, not asserted. Comfortable saying "walk me through the assumptions" and expects a real answer, not a confident summary.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Costs or resourcing needs that are underspecified; optimistic projections without a downside case; a proposal that creates financial complexity without a clear owner to manage it going forward.

**What would win them over.** A credible financial model with the assumptions shown, a stated downside scenario, and clarity on who monitors the ongoing cost once it's live.

---

## The Chief Operating Officer

**Perspective & priorities.** Holds both the mission-facing and strategic sides of operations—including family generosity services—alongside more traditional operational planning, donor care, and nonprofit-facing functions. Thinks less about owning every function personally and more about watching the broader landscape: market conditions, emerging partnerships, and whether The Signatry should build something itself or come alongside an organization already doing it well. Operates from genuine curiosity, often the one in the room asking "why are we actually doing this" before anyone gets to "how."

**Communication style.** Reflective and exploratory; comfortable sitting with an open question rather than rushing to a plan. Will slow a fast-moving proposal down with a well-placed "why," and prefers to understand purpose and direction before resources get committed.

**Typical concerns/objections.** A proposal that answers "what" and "how" but never "why"; building something internally when a partnership could meet the same need just as well; losing sight of the mission-facing, family-generosity dimension of operations in favor of pure efficiency.

**What would win them over.** A clear, honest answer to "why does this matter," a look at whether a partnership already exists to meet the need, and confirmation that the mission-facing and family-generosity implications were considered alongside the operational ones.

---

## The Chief Revenue/Advancement Officer

**Perspective & priorities.** Focused on equipping and encouraging donors and advisors toward biblically responsible giving strategies—the growth and relationship engine of the ministry. Evaluates proposals by whether they deepen trust with business owners, philanthropists, and the advisors who refer them, not just whether they generate volume.

**Communication style.** A storyteller rather than a numbers person—makes the case through the families and advisors it will affect, not through metrics. Warm and relationally attuned, and loses interest quickly if asked to engage a proposal purely as a spreadsheet.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Messaging that feels transactional or growth-for-its-own-sake; a proposal that could put an advisor or donor relationship at risk for the sake of efficiency; losing the "come alongside" posture in favor of scale.

**What would win them over.** A story that shows the proposal deepening relationship and trust, not just revenue, and a clear read on how it would land with the advisors and donor families closest to the mission.

---

## The Chief Legal Officer

**Perspective & priorities.** Structures gifts and manages legal risk—especially the negotiation of complex, nonliquid asset gifts like closely held stock and real estate. Protects both the ministry and the donor in any transaction or new process, and thinks in terms of what a proposal commits the organization to contractually or legally.

**Communication style.** Careful and precise; wants defensible, specific language rather than aspirational framing. Will flag anything vague about ownership, liability, or process before it goes further.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Undefined legal exposure in a new process or gift structure; marketing or mission language substituting for precise contractual terms; a timeline that doesn't allow for real legal review.

**What would win them over.** Clear, specific language about what the ministry and the donor are each committing to, and confirmation that legal review happens before rollout, not after.

---

## The Chief Information Officer

**Perspective & priorities.** Functions as an insider advisor—seeing the data and signals before they're released more broadly—and serves as the connective tissue that turns a decision made in one function into something actually built, secured, and adopted across the others. Focused on smart technology adoption that lets the ministry run more efficiently and scale well, weighing cybersecurity concerns pragmatically rather than treating them as a reason to stall. The internal voice pushing for thoughtful AI adoption, leaning early majority rather than bleeding edge—though with a tendency to move faster than the organization's change management can actually absorb.

**Communication style.** Systems-minded and cross-functional; translates between the language of strategy and the language of implementation. Comfortable raising a security or governance question that slows a proposal down, and just as comfortable pushing an AI or automation opportunity that no one else has considered yet.

**Typical concerns/objections.** A proposal that assumes technology or data work is trivial when it isn't; a new tool or process introduced without a cybersecurity or data-governance review; AI or automation being avoided out of blanket caution, or adopted carelessly with no guardrails.

**What would win them over.** Being looped in before a decision is finalized rather than after, a clear-eyed security and data review, and a proposal that treats AI adoption as a capability to build thoughtfully—not a shortcut and not a threat.

---

## The Chief Accounting & Compliance Officer

**Perspective & priorities.** Owns corporate accounting, payroll and benefits administration, regulatory compliance, tax, and audit. The most control- and documentation-focused voice in the room—evaluates everything by whether it holds up under an audit or a regulator's scrutiny, not by how compelling the story is.

**Communication style.** Measured, exacting, and unmoved by enthusiasm alone. Wants documentation and a clear regulatory read before signing off on anything new, and will say so plainly even when it's the least popular voice in the room.

**Typical concerns/objections.** A new process introduced without clear controls or an audit trail; regulatory or tax implications not addressed up front; assuming compliance will "work itself out" once something has already launched.

**What would win them over.** A clear compliance and controls review completed before rollout, documented processes, and a straight answer about regulatory exposure rather than reassurance without specifics.

---

## The Chief People & Culture Officer

**Perspective & priorities.** Owns talent, staff capacity, and organizational culture—the internal counterpart to the Chief Revenue/Advancement Officer's external relationship focus. Evaluates every proposal by whether the people actually doing the work have the capacity, skill, and support to execute it well, not just whether the strategy is sound on paper. Protective of staff wellbeing, and mindful that repeated fast pivots erode trust and morale over time even when each one makes sense on its own.

**Communication style.** Warm but direct; asks practical questions about workload, training, and support before getting swept up in a new initiative's momentum. Will name burnout risk plainly, even when it's the least welcome thing to raise in a room full of enthusiasm.

**Typical concerns/objections.** A rollout timeline that assumes staff can absorb change on top of an already full workload; no plan for training or communication to the team actually executing it; a pattern of fast pivots that leaves the same people covering the gap every time.

**What would win them over.** A realistic timeline that accounts for current staff capacity, a clear training and communication plan for the team who will execute it, and evidence that the pace of change is sustainable rather than just exciting.

---

## The VP of Family Generosity Services

**Perspective & priorities.** Focused on equipping and encouraging donor families to wrestle with what God has entrusted to them and how to model that stewardship to current and future generations. Evaluates every proposal by whether it deepens a family's discipleship journey around generosity, not just whether it grows giving volume or improves donor-experience efficiency. The most explicitly pastoral voice in the room.

**Communication style.** Warm, reflective, and relationally patient; comfortable sitting with a family's spiritual questions rather than rushing to a giving strategy. Speaks in terms of formation and legacy, not metrics or mechanics.

**Typical concerns/objections.** A proposal that treats family generosity services as a donor-retention tactic rather than a genuine discipleship ministry; language or process that rushes a family past reflection toward a transaction; losing the multigenerational, formational purpose in favor of efficiency or scale.

**What would win them over.** Evidence that a proposal serves a family's actual spiritual and relational journey—not just their giving capacity—and space in the process for reflection rather than immediate action.

## Using these nine together

- **Mission story vs. hard numbers.** The CEO and Chief Revenue/Advancement Officer test a proposal against mission and relationship; the CFO and Chief Accounting & Compliance Officer test it against sustainability and controls. A pitch that only works as a story, or only works as a spreadsheet, will stall with the other half of the room.
- **Growth vs. protection.** The Chief Revenue/Advancement Officer wants to deepen relationships and grow; the Chief Legal Officer and Chief Accounting & Compliance Officer want to make sure growth doesn't outrun what can be defended legally or in an audit. Both are needed before something is genuinely ready to launch.
- **Why vs. how.** The COO asks why a proposal matters before engaging its mechanics; the CIO asks how it actually gets built, secured, and adopted across every function it touches. A proposal that skips straight to implementation without a clear "why" will stall with the COO even if the CIO finds it technically sound.
- **Founder energy vs. institutional guardrails.** The CEO's entrepreneurial instinct to move fast is the natural counterweight to the Chief Accounting & Compliance Officer's insistence on controls and documentation—a healthy proposal survives contact with both, not just the one that happens to be in the room.
- **Momentum vs. capacity.** The CEO and CIO both push for speed—the CEO for mission and market reasons, the CIO because pushing technology forward naturally outpaces what change management can absorb. The Chief People & Culture Officer is the check on whether the staff actually executing the work can absorb that pace without burning out.
- **Growth engine vs. discipleship depth.** The Chief Revenue/Advancement Officer evaluates a donor-facing idea by whether it builds trust and referral growth; the VP of Family Generosity Services evaluates the same idea by whether it actually deepens a family's formation in generosity. An initiative optimized purely for acquisition can look great to one and hollow to the other.
- **The real filter question.** Assume every shepherd will eventually ask some version of "who owns this, and what does it cost us—in dollars, relationships, security, staff capacity, or mission clarity—if we get it wrong."
