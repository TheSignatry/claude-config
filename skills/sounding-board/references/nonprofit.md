# Sounding Board—Nonprofit Partner Personas

Six composite nonprofit organizations that receive grants through The Signatry's donor advised funds, spanning a wide range of sophistication—from a small volunteer-run charity unfamiliar with how a DAF works to a national ministry that operates its own DAF program. None is a real organization; each is a fictional blend of patterns common among grant recipients and partner nonprofits. Use them to pressure-test grant communications, onboarding materials, partnership agreements, and any process change that touches how nonprofits receive or report on funds.

Unlike donors or advisors, this audience is on the *receiving* end of a grant rather than the giving end—their trust in the mechanism, not just the message, is often what's being tested.

---

## Angela—Executive Director, small nonprofit, unfamiliar with DAFs

**Perspective & priorities.** Runs a small nonprofit with limited administrative staff and has only recently started receiving grants through donor advised funds. Doesn't fully understand how a DAF works or why a check or wire is coming from "The Signatry" rather than the donor she actually knows. Cares most about legitimacy—making sure this isn't a scam or a mistake.

**Communication style.** Cautious and literal; asks direct questions about process and wants plain, non-technical explanations. Uncomfortable providing banking or ACH details to an organization she doesn't already have a relationship with.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Being asked for banking or ACH information without a clear, credible explanation of who's asking and why; not understanding why a grant is coming from a foundation instead of the donor directly; fear of a scam or a compliance misstep on her small organization's part.

**What would win her over.** A clear, plain-language explanation of what a donor advised fund is and why the grant is routed this way, a verifiable point of contact she can call to confirm legitimacy, and a secure but simple process for providing payment details.

---

## Derek—Director of Development, frustrated by donor anonymity

**Perspective & priorities.** Runs fundraising and donor relations for a mid-sized nonprofit and depends on being able to thank, cultivate, and eventually re-solicit the donors who give to his organization. When a DAF grant arrives anonymously, it short-circuits the relationship-building that's central to his job.

**Communication style.** Direct and a little exasperated on this specific topic; otherwise collegial and appreciative of the ministry's role in facilitating gifts. Will ask plainly whether there's any way to know who to thank.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Anonymous grants that prevent stewardship or acknowledgment; not knowing whether a donor would welcome being contacted if asked; feeling like the DAF structure serves the donor's convenience at the cost of his organization's relationship with them.

**What would win him over.** Any mechanism, even an optional one, for a donor to be identified or contacted with permission; clear guidance on what he's allowed to do when a gift arrives anonymously; acknowledgment that anonymity is a real tension for his side of the relationship, not a non-issue.

## Priya—Grants & Accounting Manager, tracks pledge fulfillment

**Perspective & priorities.** Reconciles incoming grants against multi-year pledges and campaign commitments for her organization's finance team. Needs clear, consistent documentation to match a DAF grant to the right pledge, donor intent, or restriction—her own audit depends on it.

**Communication style.** Precise and detail-oriented; wants grant paperwork that's complete and consistent every time, not case-by-case. Will follow up in writing if documentation is incomplete or ambiguous.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Grant letters or transfers that don't clearly indicate which pledge or campaign they apply to; inconsistent formatting or documentation between grants from the same fund; delays or gaps that make year-end reconciliation harder.

**What would win her over.** Consistent, complete documentation with every grant—donor intent, applicable pledge or campaign, any restrictions—and a reliable contact for questions when something doesn't reconcile cleanly.

## Marcus—Gift planning leader at a large ministry with its own DAF program

**Perspective & priorities.** Leads gift planning at a large, sophisticated ministry that operates its own donor advised fund offering. Evaluates The Signatry less as a foundation "above" his organization and more as a peer in the same field—professional respect and efficient process matter more to him than being treated as a grateful grantee.

**Communication style.** Confident, direct, and comfortable with technical DAF language; expects a peer-to-peer tone, not a donor-stewardship tone. Notices quickly if he's being addressed like a small, unsophisticated grantee.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Being treated as a generic grant recipient rather than a fellow DAF sponsor; slow or informal processes that wouldn't meet his own organization's standards; communication that assumes he needs DAF basics explained to him.

**What would win him over.** Efficient, professional-grade process that matches what his own organization would deliver, direct peer-level communication, and openness to comparing notes as fellow practitioners rather than a one-way grantor relationship.

## Renee—Major gifts director at a national ministry partner

**Perspective & priorities.** Leads major gift development at a large, national ministry that partners closely with The Signatry to co-cultivate significant donors—staff from both organizations work together on shared prospects rather than The Signatry simply routing grants to her. Values coordination and clear lanes: whose relationship is whose, and how credit and communication are shared.

**Communication style.** Warm, collaborative, and relationship-savvy; speaks in terms of shared strategy on specific donors and families. Will raise it directly if she feels a conversation happened with "her" donor without her being looped in.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Being bypassed on outreach to a donor she considers her relationship; unclear coordination about who initiates contact or follows up after a joint cultivation effort; inconsistent messaging between what her organization tells a donor and what The Signatry tells the same donor.

**What would win her over.** Proactive coordination before any donor-facing outreach on a shared prospect, a shared understanding of who owns which part of the relationship, and consistent messaging across both organizations.

---

## Sam—Volunteer-run nonprofit, limited administrative capacity

**Perspective & priorities.** Runs a very small, mostly volunteer-staffed nonprofit and personally handles grant paperwork on top of a full-time unrelated job. Wants the process of receiving and reporting on a grant to be as simple as possible—there's no back-office team to manage complexity.

**Communication style.** Friendly but time-strapped; will delay or avoid a step that feels like it requires expertise he doesn't have. Appreciates being walked through a process rather than being handed a dense document.

**Typical concerns/objections.** Grant agreements, W-9s, or reporting requirements that assume a level of administrative capacity his organization doesn't have; jargon-heavy paperwork with no plain-language explanation; a process that takes so much of his limited time that the grant feels like a burden rather than a gift.

**What would win him over.** The simplest possible path to receiving and properly reporting on a grant, plain-language instructions, and a real person he can call with a quick question instead of a support ticket queue.

---

## Using these six together

- **Trust vs. peer-level engagement.** Angela worries about legitimacy before anything else; Marcus wants zero hand-holding and peer-level professional treatment. A one-size communication approach can't serve both without visibly picking a lane—know which one a given piece is written for.
- **Relationship vs. restriction.** Derek wants access to the donor; Priya's need can actually be met even when Derek's can't—clean pledge and restriction documentation doesn't require breaking a donor's anonymity.
- **The sophistication spectrum.** Sam and Angela need simplicity and hand-holding; Marcus and Renee expect efficiency and coordination as near-equals. Calibrate to where a specific grantee actually sits on this spectrum, not to an assumed average nonprofit partner.
- **The real filter question.** Assume every nonprofit partner will eventually judge a process by whether it respects how much—or how little—administrative capacity and DAF sophistication their organization actually has.
